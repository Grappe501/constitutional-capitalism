# The Declaration of Constitutional Capitalism

**Status:** First draft — governing philosophical foundation in development. Not a final ratified text.  
**Version:** 0.1.0-draft  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-04

> Constitutional Capitalism is an economic philosophy in which free markets, private property, entrepreneurship, and innovation are protected by constitutional principles that ensure economic power remains accountable to the people. It recognizes that wealth is most effectively created through free enterprise, but that lasting prosperity depends upon broad opportunity, meaningful competition, responsible ownership, and institutions that prevent the excessive concentration of economic or political power.

> The purpose of an economy is not merely to create wealth, but to create a prosperous, free, and self-governing people.

---

## I. Preamble

A free people cannot remain free for long if their economic lives are governed by powers they cannot understand, contest, or restrain. Political constitutions were written because political power concentrates, and concentrated power — even when claimed in the name of the people — tends toward abuse. The same human tendency appears in economic life. Markets create abundance. Ownership creates independence. Enterprise creates discovery. Yet when economic power gathers without transparent limits, it can narrow opportunity, capture government, dominate communities, and leave citizens formally free while practically dependent.

This Declaration does not call for the abolition of markets, the confiscation of property, or the replacement of enterprise by central command. It calls for something more disciplined and more durable: a constitutional framework for economic power that preserves free enterprise while preventing either the state or concentrated private institutions from ruling without accountability.

What is at risk is not only income or employment. What is at risk is the material independence of free citizens; the vitality of towns and local economies; the integrity of competition; the dignity of work; and the capacity of a people to govern themselves without becoming clients of distant corporations, financial intermediaries, digital platforms, or bureaucratic authorities.

What must be preserved is equally clear. Private property. Free enterprise. Entrepreneurship. Lawful profit. Voluntary exchange. Innovation. Investment. Limited government. The rule of law. These are not secondary ornaments of prosperity. They are foundations of liberty.

Reform must therefore occur without revolution. A free republic does not secure justice by destroying the productive order that feeds, employs, invents, and invests. It secures justice by writing better rules, enforcing them openly, phasing change predictably, and measuring results honestly. Economic power is a constitutional concern because institutions that control livelihoods, information, capital, market access, and political influence can shape the conditions of freedom as surely as statutes and elections do.

Free people require both political and economic independence. The ballot without the means to live with dignity becomes fragile. Property without competition and accountability can harden into domination. Constitutional Capitalism holds these truths together.

---

## II. The Purpose of an Economy

An economy is a human institution. It exists to serve human life, not to convert human life into a residual after markets have finished celebrating themselves.

Economic success must therefore be evaluated by whether it supports human dignity, individual liberty, material security, broad opportunity, family stability, community life, democratic self-government, and intergenerational progress. Gross domestic product, corporate profit, stock-market growth, and aggregate wealth are important indicators. They tell us something real about productive capacity and capital formation. They do not, by themselves, tell us whether a free people is becoming more capable of self-rule or more dependent on powers beyond their reach.

A nation may grow richer in aggregate while many of its citizens lose ownership, bargaining power, local economic centers, and confidence that honest work leads to a durable stake in the future. In such a case, the indicators of growth have not failed entirely — but they have failed as a complete measure of national prosperity.

Constitutional Capitalism therefore begins with a purpose statement before it turns to instruments. Markets are means. Ownership is a means. Tax policy is a means. Antitrust is a means. The end is a prosperous, free, and self-governing people.

This does not romanticize poverty, nor does it treat success as suspect. It insists that the legitimacy of an economic order is measured not only by how much wealth it creates, but by whether the conditions of freedom and broad opportunity remain intact while that wealth is created.

---

## III. What Constitutional Capitalism Preserves

Constitutional Capitalism is a market philosophy. It preserves private property as a foundation of economic freedom and personal independence. It preserves free enterprise as the most effective engine of discovery, coordination, and wealth creation yet known. It preserves entrepreneurship, lawful profit, voluntary exchange, consumer choice, innovation, investment, competition, personal initiative, and savings.

It also preserves inheritance within constitutional limits — not as an unlimited claim against the public interest, but as a recognition that families transmit knowledge, capital, and responsibility across generations. It preserves limited government, the rule of law, due process, freedom of association, and freedom of contract subject to constitutional boundaries that protect liberty rather than annihilate it.

These preservations are not rhetorical comforts offered before a program of destruction. They are structural commitments. A philosophy that cannot protect enterprise cannot protect liberty. A philosophy that cannot protect property cannot protect independence. A philosophy that treats profit itself as injustice will soon treat productive excellence as a political offense.

Constitutional Capitalism therefore rejects hostility toward business success as such. It rejects the fantasy that abundance can be commanded into existence by decree. It rejects the idea that the path to justice lies through the permanent politicization of every economic decision.

What it seeks to restrain is not wealth creation, but unaccountable power.

---

## IV. What Constitutional Capitalism Changes

Preservation without reform is nostalgia. Reform without preservation is ruin. Constitutional Capitalism changes the terms under which economic power operates in a free republic.

It holds that economic power must remain constitutionally accountable. Markets are not natural weather systems that appear without law. They are created, protected, and structured by legal institutions: property rights, contract enforcement, corporate forms, bankruptcy, securities rules, competition law, and public infrastructure. Because markets depend on law, the design of that law is a public question.

Corporations receive legal privileges — limited liability, perpetual existence, access to courts, capital markets, intellectual-property protection, and the benefits of public infrastructure and educated labor. Privileges of that scale create reciprocal public obligations. This is not a claim that every firm is a government agency. It is a claim that legal creations of public-scale power cannot pretend to be purely private when their conduct shapes the lives of millions.

Work should create a pathway to ownership and security, not merely a cycle of wages insufficient to accumulate a stake in the productive order. Concentrated power must be checked whether it is public or private. Competition must be protected as a constitutional safeguard, not treated as an optional efficiency preference. Prosperity must circulate through workers and communities if a republic is to remain socially coherent and politically free.

Taxation should avoid punishing ordinary productive labor as though work were a wrongdoing. Businesses that use public systems should help sustain them. Market access creates responsibilities. Technological productivity should benefit the public broadly, not only those who capture the final layer of returns. Local communities are economic participants and civic institutions, not disposable locations to be exhausted and abandoned without consequence or transition.

These changes do not require pretending that every future policy is already known. They require a governing philosophy capable of evaluating future policies.

## V. Rights and Responsibilities

A constitutional economic order must describe rights and responsibilities together. Rights without responsibilities become claims against reality. Responsibilities without rights become obedience.

### Individuals

Individuals have rights to own property; to work; to create; to organize; to invest; to establish enterprises; to enter voluntary agreements; to retain meaningful rewards from productive effort; to receive due process; and to participate in economic governance where their rights are materially affected.

They also have responsibilities to comply with fair laws; to respect the rights of others; to honor lawful obligations; to avoid fraud, coercion, and exploitation; and to contribute reasonably to the institutions that protect liberty and commerce.

### Workers

Workers have rights to fair and transparent compensation; safe conditions; freedom of association; protection from arbitrary coercion; portable economic security; participation in productivity gains; meaningful paths toward ownership; and access to understandable employment terms.

They have responsibilities to perform agreed work honestly; to protect legitimate organizational interests; to maintain professional standards; to contribute to shared productivity; and to respect lawful agreements.

These statements are philosophical and institutional before they are statutory. Not every item listed here is, by that listing alone, an immediately enforceable legal right in every jurisdiction. Distinguishing philosophical rights, institutional goals, and possible statutory rights is part of intellectual honesty. A republic that confuses aspiration with enacted law invites both cynicism and overreach.

### Owners and Investors

Owners and investors have rights to establish businesses; to direct lawful enterprise; to earn profit; to receive returns; to control property within lawful boundaries; to take legitimate economic risks; and to protect intellectual and physical property.

They have responsibilities to bear genuine risk; to avoid externalizing preventable costs onto workers, communities, or the public; to treat workers and communities as contributors rather than disposable inputs; to disclose material economic conduct where public-scale power is exercised; to avoid monopoly abuse; and to sustain the obligations attached to limited liability and market access.

Profit is not theft. Externalizing harm while privatizing gain is not free enterprise in any morally serious sense.

### Corporations

Corporations are created and protected by law. Their privileges may include limited liability, perpetual existence, access to public courts, intellectual-property protection, capital markets, public infrastructure, educated labor, currency stability, and market access. Those privileges create reciprocal responsibilities of transparency, fair dealing, competition, and contribution to the public systems that make large-scale enterprise possible.

A corporation is not a citizen. It is a legal instrument. When that instrument becomes large enough to shape markets, information, labor conditions, or politics, the public has a legitimate interest in the rules under which it operates.

### Government

Government must preserve constitutional liberty; enforce contracts; protect property; defend competition; prevent fraud and coercion; limit monopoly; maintain public infrastructure; protect the integrity of markets; ensure transparency; prevent economic power from capturing public institutions; and remain constrained, reviewable, transparent, and accountable.

Government must not control ordinary production without compelling necessity; arbitrarily seize property; reward political allies; suppress lawful enterprise; centralize power merely because private concentration exists; use economic regulation to eliminate dissent; create permanent dependence where ownership and independence can be built; or substitute bureaucratic preference for every private decision.

The cure for unaccountable private power is not unaccountable state power. The cure is constitutional structure.

## VI. The Constitutional Limits on Economic Power

Political constitutions divide, enumerate, and restrain power because human beings cannot safely be trusted with unlimited authority. Economic institutions require a related discipline.

The governing principle is this:

> Power that can materially control the lives, opportunities, speech, livelihood, information, or political influence of millions of people must be subject to transparent and enforceable limits.

This principle applies to monopoly and monopsony; to control of essential infrastructure; to financial concentration; to digital-platform control; to employer domination in isolated labor markets; to information concentration; to corporate influence over government; and to government capture by private interests.

Corporations and governments are not identical. Governments claim coercive authority backed by law. Corporations ordinarily operate through property, contract, employment, and market position. That distinction matters. It does not dissolve the reality that private institutions can acquire coercive or quasi-coercive power when exit is costly, alternatives are few, information is controlled, livelihoods are gated, or public rules are shaped by the powerful themselves.

Constitutional Capitalism therefore refuses two comforting myths. The first myth is that whatever emerges from a market is automatically free of domination. The second myth is that whatever is done by the state is automatically done for the people. Both myths have produced avoidable suffering.

Limits on economic power must be transparent, lawful, reviewable, and oriented toward preserving competition, ownership, and self-government — not toward installing a permanent managerial class that replaces private concentration with bureaucratic command.

---

## VII. Broad Ownership

Broad ownership is the central economic mechanism of Constitutional Capitalism.

A republic that waits until wealth has already concentrated and then attempts to repair the damage solely through after-the-fact redistribution will live in permanent conflict. Redistribution may sometimes be necessary. It is a weak substitute for participation in wealth creation.

Constitutional Capitalism seeks to broaden participation through employee ownership, retirement ownership, cooperative ownership, homeownership, local enterprise, entrepreneurship, community investment, social wealth mechanisms, shared productivity gains, and access to capital. The mix of mechanisms may vary by nation, sector, and circumstance. The principle does not vary: free citizens need durable stakes in the productive order.

> Wages support life. Ownership builds independence.

Wages matter. Fair compensation is not optional morality; it is part of a just exchange. But wages alone rarely create the resilience that ownership creates. Ownership builds durable wealth, strengthens citizenship, reduces dependence, distributes economic influence, gives families resilience, and connects technological progress to public prosperity.

Broad ownership is not guaranteed equality of outcome. It is a commitment to institutions that make ownership attainable for those who work, save, create, and contribute — rather than confining productive capital to a narrowing circle while the majority is told that freedom consists of choosing among employers they cannot influence and products they cannot own.

---

## VIII. Competition as a Constitutional Safeguard

Competition is often discussed as a technical condition for lower prices and higher efficiency. It is that. It is also more than that.

Competition disperses power. It protects consumer choice. It creates space for entrepreneurs. It strengthens labor bargaining by multiplying alternatives. It limits political influence by reducing dependence on single institutions. It encourages innovation by denying incumbents the quiet life of uncontested control.

When competition collapses, citizens do not merely pay more. They bargain from weakness, work under narrowed options, receive information through fewer gatekeepers, and face corporations or platforms large enough to shape the political environment itself.

Antitrust and competition policy therefore belong to the republic’s power-balancing architecture. They are not left-wing ornaments or right-wing inconveniences. They are instruments for keeping markets worthy of the name.

Constitutional Capitalism does not treat every large firm as illegitimate. Scale can serve consumers and enable complex production. The question is whether scale remains exposed to rivalry, entry, substitution, and rule-bound accountability — or whether it becomes a private government without a constitution.

---

## IX. Work and Human Dignity

Work is not merely an input in someone else’s production function. It is one of the principal ways free people contribute to the world, support families, develop skill, and claim a rightful share in prosperity.

Productive work should not be treated as taxable wrongdoing. Workers are contributors to value creation. Productivity gains should not flow exclusively upward as though capital alone produced them. Labor markets must remain genuinely competitive. Work should provide a plausible path toward stability and ownership. Unpaid caregiving and civic contribution also sustain society, even when they do not appear neatly in market prices.

Human worth does not depend exclusively on employment. A free society must not reduce persons to their wage status. But a society that systematically disconnects work from security, ownership, and respect will not remain free in any thick sense of the word. Dependence is not dignity. Neither is contempt for those who work.

---

## X. Communities as Economic Institutions

Communities are not scenic backgrounds for corporate strategy. They provide workers, customers, roads, schools, public safety, courts, utilities, culture, stability, and legitimacy. They educate children, house families, and absorb the social consequences of economic decisions made elsewhere.

Businesses may relocate. Capital is mobile. Communities bear the losses when productive centers disappear: emptied storefronts, shrinking tax bases, weakened schools, stranded workers, and civic demoralization. A philosophy that notices only national aggregates will miss this human geography.

Constitutional Capitalism recognizes local wealth retention, local ownership, community transition rights, responsible corporate exit, locally returned taxation, public investment, rural economic access, and the value of physical commercial presence. It does not require every firm to remain forever in one place. It does require that market access and public privilege be accompanied by rules that take community consequences seriously.

Localism can become inefficient or exclusionary if it hardens into hostility toward outsiders or denial of beneficial trade. That risk must be faced honestly. The opposite risk — treating places as disposable — has already been faced by too many communities.

---

## XI. Technology and the National Inheritance

Modern innovation builds upon publicly funded research, inherited scientific knowledge, infrastructure, educational institutions, accumulated social knowledge, and generations of workers and inventors. Private innovators deserve rewards. No generation creates prosperity alone.

Artificial intelligence and automation intensify an old question under new conditions:

> When publicly accumulated knowledge and national infrastructure produce extraordinary private productivity, how should the gains be divided among innovators, investors, workers, communities, and future generations?

This Declaration does not prescribe the final formula. It establishes the principle that technological productivity is not solely a private windfall detached from the public inheritance that made it possible. Shared prosperity in an age of intelligent machines will require ownership pathways, competitive markets, educational access, and institutions that prevent a narrow capture of gains that were socially prepared over decades.

To deny innovators rewards is to kill discovery. To deny the public any claim on the fruits of a shared inheritance is to convert the commonwealth into a private enclosure.

---

## XII. Taxation and Public Obligation

Taxation is one of the most powerful instruments of economic constitution. It can protect productive effort or punish it. It can sustain public goods or become an opaque maze of privilege. It can fall where statutes pretend it falls, or elsewhere through incidence.

Constitutional Capitalism advances principles before rates.

Ordinary work should carry the lightest reasonable burden. Survival necessities should not be heavily taxed. Economic rents can bear greater taxation than productive effort. Entities that benefit most from public systems should help sustain those systems. Taxes should be transparent. Taxes should minimize avoidance and arbitrary complexity. Market access can create tax obligations. Local and internet commerce should face equal product-tax treatment. Tax structures should be evaluated by actual incidence, not merely by the statutory payer. Taxation should not quietly shift corporate obligations onto workers and consumers while congratulating itself for having taxed “business.”

These are evaluative principles. They do not yet settle the design of any particular code. They do establish that a philosophy claiming to honor work cannot treat labor as the most convenient place to extract revenue while mobile capital and concentrated rents arrange exits.

---

## XIII. Reform Without Revolution

The justice of an aim does not redeem the recklessness of a method.

Constitutional Capitalism rejects confiscatory chaos; sudden abolition of markets; arbitrary nationalization; political punishment; destruction of retirement savings; destabilization of small businesses; legal uncertainty; and class retaliation. A free people do not build a better economic order by humiliating producers, terrifying savers, or inviting capital flight through theatrical unpredictability.

Reform should proceed through law, democratic consent, predictable transition, prospective rules, due process, testing, phased implementation, measurement, amendment, and correction. Where evidence is incomplete, humility is a constitutional virtue. Where power is concentrated, delay can also be a form of injustice. The task is to move with both courage and care.

Revolution promises purification and often delivers new masters. Constitutional reform promises something less intoxicating and more adult: rules that can be argued about, enacted, tested, and revised without burning the productive inheritance of a nation.

---

## XIV. A Framework Adaptable Across Nations

Constitutional Capitalism is not a proprietary American product. It is a framework of principles that different constitutional republics may adapt according to their constitutional structure, legal traditions, federal or unitary governance, economic development, labor institutions, existing social systems, cultural expectations, and international obligations.

Universal principles should be distinguished from national implementation examples. The United States may illustrate certain problems and institutions; it does not exhaust the meaning of the philosophy. A nation with different labor law, different corporate traditions, or different social insurance arrangements may pursue broad ownership, competition, and accountable economic power through different instruments.

What should travel across borders is the core proposition: free enterprise and constitutional accountability belong together. What should remain adaptable is the statutory and institutional design through which that proposition becomes real.

## Additional Clarifications for Readers and Future Editors

### On wealth and justice

Constitutional Capitalism does not treat wealth as evidence of vice. A free society should hope for creators, builders, savers, and investors who succeed. The moral problem is not that some people become rich through productive contribution. The moral problem arises when rules, privileges, barriers, captured regulation, or collapsed competition convert success into unaccountable dominion over the lives of others. Distinguishing earned productive success from entrenched domination is one of the central intellectual tasks of this project. That distinction will require evidence in later phases. It begins here as a principle of judgment.

### On equality

This philosophy does not promise equality of outcome. People differ in talent, preference, luck, diligence, risk tolerance, and timing. A free economy will produce unequal results. What it must not produce, if it wishes to remain compatible with self-government, is a closed structure in which opportunity is formally announced and practically rationed by concentrated gatekeepers. Broad ownership, competition, and accountable institutions are instruments against closure, not instruments of engineered sameness.

### On the corporation as legal technology

The corporation is one of the most powerful legal technologies ever invented. It pools capital, limits liability, endures beyond founders, and enables projects no lone household could finance. Constitutional Capitalism does not seek to abolish that technology. It seeks to remember what it is: a public legal invention serving public and private purposes under rules. When corporate form becomes a shield for conduct that would be intolerable if undertaken by a named person with no privilege, the constitutional question reappears. Privilege and obligation travel together.

### On information power

In earlier centuries, domination often required land, armies, or factories. In the present century, domination may also operate through platforms that mediate speech, commerce, reputation, employment matching, and political attention. Constitutional Capitalism does not equate every large digital platform with a government. It does insist that institutions controlling essential channels of economic and civic participation cannot be analyzed solely as ordinary shops selling ordinary goods. Scale plus indispensability plus opacity creates a constitutional problem even when the legal form remains private.

### On the poor and the precarious

A philosophy of ownership that speaks only to those already near ownership is incomplete. Pathways into property, skill, savings, and enterprise must be real for people now living close to the edge. This does not require pretending that every person will become an entrepreneur. It requires that the baseline of work, housing, and portable security not be designed as a permanent trap. Later policy chapters will wrestle with instruments. The Declaration establishes the moral direction: independence is preferable to administered dependence wherever independence can be honestly built.

### On international commerce

Constitutional Capitalism is not a doctrine of sealed borders or economic solitude. Lawful international commerce can widen opportunity, diffuse knowledge, and discipline domestic incumbents. The question is whether trade and capital mobility operate under rules that preserve domestic capacity for self-government, fair competition, and community viability — or whether they become pathways for regulatory arbitrage, labor arbitrage without reciprocal obligation, and the permanent export of civic consequence. Universal principles can condemn predation without condemning exchange.

### On evidence and humility

Many readers will ask for proof. They should. Claims about wage-productivity divergence, monopoly harms, ownership effects, tax incidence, and democratic erosion are empirical questions as well as moral ones. This Declaration states principles and directions. It does not pretend that the empirical case is finished. Unsupported claims must remain labeled. Sources must be gathered. Models must be tested. Opponents must be answered at their strongest. A philosophy unwilling to survive disagreement does not deserve to govern a free people.

### On the relationship between this Declaration and future Articles

The Economic Articles sketched elsewhere in the project are not yet law and are not yet final doctrine. They are an emerging institutional architecture. This Declaration is prior to them. If an Article cannot be justified by the principles here — or contradicts them — the Article must change, not the other way around. Philosophy before program is the discipline that prevents Constitutional Capitalism from collapsing into a bag of disconnected proposals.

### On tone and public trust

This project will fail if it becomes a vehicle for contempt — contempt for workers, contempt for owners, contempt for the poor, contempt for the successful, or contempt for those who disagree. Free people persuade. They measure. They amend. They do not require unanimous enthusiasm before they require honest argument. The Declaration is written to be sharable across disagreement precisely because the stakes are shared: whether the next generation inherits a republic of owners and citizens, or a landscape of dependents managed by powers they did not choose and cannot effectively restrain.

### On the meaning of constitutional accountability

Constitutional accountability does not mean that every firm must await permission before innovating. It means that when institutions acquire power capable of shaping livelihoods, market access, information flows, or political influence at public scale, the rules governing that power must be knowable, contestable, and enforceable. Hidden privilege is not liberty. Arbitrary discretion — public or private — is not law.

### On savings, pensions, and ordinary capital

Millions of citizens encounter capitalism primarily through wages, housing, and retirement accounts. Any reform that casually destabilizes those forms of ownership betrays the people it claims to help. Constitutional Capitalism therefore treats the protection of lawful savings and retirement ownership as a transition constraint, not as an afterthought. Broadening ownership cannot begin by wrecking the ownership that already exists for ordinary families.

### On small enterprise

Small businesses are not miniature versions of concentrated platforms. They are often civic infrastructure: employers of neighbors, sponsors of local life, and repositories of practical knowledge. A philosophy obsessed only with the largest firms will misunderstand the economy. Rules aimed at public-scale power must be designed so they do not crush the shop, the clinic, the farm, the contractor, or the local manufacturer under compliance burdens meant for giants.


### On education and capability

Ownership without capability can become a slogan. A free people need education that forms not only employees, but owners, citizens, and stewards: financial literacy, civic literacy, practical skill, and the habits of responsibility. Constitutional Capitalism does not reduce education to job training, nor does it pretend schooling alone can substitute for fair markets and accountable institutions. It does insist that broad prosperity requires people prepared to understand contracts, investments, enterprises, and public choices.

### On measurement

What a nation measures, it tends to manage. If the only celebrated numbers are aggregate output and asset prices, then policy will drift toward those numbers even when independence and community vitality decline. Constitutional Capitalism therefore anticipates later work on better measures of national prosperity — not to abolish GDP, but to stop mistaking one instrument panel for the whole machine. Measurement reform belongs downstream of purpose. Purpose has been stated here.

### On disagreement inside the project

Reasonable supporters of Constitutional Capitalism will disagree about tax design, ownership mechanisms, antitrust remedies, transition speed, and the precise edge between private liberty and public obligation. That disagreement is not failure. It is evidence that the philosophy is a framework for argument rather than a personality cult. What must remain shared is the refusal of two extremes: markets without constitutional accountability, and states without constitutional limits.

## XV. The Declaration

We believe that the purpose of an economy is not merely to create wealth, but to create a prosperous, free, and self-governing people.

We affirm private property, free enterprise, entrepreneurship, lawful profit, voluntary exchange, innovation, and competition as foundations of material liberty.

We affirm that markets require law, and that law must protect liberty rather than entrench domination.

We affirm that corporations created by law and privileged by law carry public obligations proportionate to their public-scale power.

We affirm the dignity of work and the right of working people to fair dealing, transparent terms, and pathways toward ownership and security.

We affirm that ownership builds independence, and that a republic of permanent non-owners is a republic at risk.

We affirm that competition is a constitutional safeguard because it disperses power and preserves choice.

We affirm that communities are economic institutions whose vitality is part of national prosperity.

We affirm that technological progress rests on a national inheritance and must serve broad prosperity as well as private reward.

We affirm limited government that is capable in its proper functions and restrained in its temptations.

We reject the concentration of unaccountable private power that narrows opportunity, captures public institutions, and converts free citizens into dependents.

We reject the concentration of unaccountable state power that claims to liberate people by managing their economic lives without end.

We reject both the romanticism of markets without rules and the romanticism of states without limits.

We reject reform by chaos, confiscation without due process, class retaliation, and the destruction of productive order in the name of justice.

We hold that economic liberty cannot survive indefinitely when economic power becomes unaccountable, and that political liberty cannot survive indefinitely when citizens lack the economic independence necessary to govern themselves.

We commit to building Constitutional Capitalism as a disciplined philosophy of free enterprise under constitutional restraint — open to evidence, open to correction, hostile to unaccountable power, and devoted to the lasting prosperity of free people.
