# Community Safety, Justice, and Restoration

**Domain status:** priority_phase_2_architecture  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-04  
**Phase:** 2 — Diagnosis Research Foundation (complete-system architecture; sourced modules continue in 2.1)

## Central question

Does the community safety and justice system prevent harm, respond quickly, protect victims, treat addiction and mental illness, hold dangerous people securely, restore people who can be restored, and return them to productive citizenship when safe — or does it function as a profit center, political weapon, or permanent system of social exclusion that makes liberty and ownership conditional on wealth?

## Why it matters

Justice is the mechanism that determines whether liberty, ownership, opportunity, and accountability are real—or merely promised. Isolated police or prison reforms are not enough. Economic diagnosis alone cannot answer whether power returns to the people if the safety system can destroy freedom, family, property, employment, housing, voting power, or future through poverty, unaffordable counsel, fees, or lifetime exclusion — or if armed officers remain the default answer to every social and health crisis.

## Strongest supporting evidence

- Community-policing three-element definition is primary federal guidance (`CC-SRC-017`).
- Federal private-prison contracting posture has reversed across administrations (`CC-SRC-018`, `CC-SRC-019`), supporting the case for settling the no-confinement-profit principle in law rather than memo cycles.
- Federal justice agencies have long supported treatment-court and justice–behavioral-health collaboration models; quality depends on design (`CC-SRC-020`, `CC-SRC-021`).
- Reentry is a continuum from intake; success metrics should be more nuanced than rearrest alone (`CC-SRC-023`); short-term reentry-service effects are often modest/mixed (`CC-SRC-024`).

## Contrary evidence

- Public-safety and incapacitation literatures argue some offenses require secure confinement; reform that ignores violence or predation can harm victims and communities.
- Restorative processes are not appropriate or safe in every case; victim participation must remain voluntary.
- Treatment and mental-health courts can risk net-widening if poorly designed; they are not universal substitutes for prosecution.
- Measurement of rehabilitation success and recidivism is contested (definitions, follow-up windows, selection).
- Local control of policing can improve accountability **or** enable rights violations or favoritism without constitutional floors and anti-nepotism safeguards.
- Some argue private corrections expand capacity faster; comparative quality evidence is contested and jurisdiction-specific.

## Uncertainties

- Causal identification of cash-bail and public-defense funding effects varies by jurisdiction and study design.
- Optimal mix of prevention, diversion, incarceration, and supervision is offense- and community-specific.
- Community Safety Corps civilianization and co-responder staffing require local capacity research (especially rural clinician supply).
- Record-sealing / reentry policy effects on employment and recidivism need careful sourced review.
- State/local private-jail and private-probation fee structures need separate modules beyond federal BOP memos.

## Constitutional implications

Developing doctrine holds that public safety belongs to the community; equal justice cannot depend primarily on wealth; punishment must be proportionate; institutions must not profit from punishment or confinement volume; victims must be supported; reentry into citizenship should be the default after lawful completion of sentence, with risk-specific reviewable exceptions; and Community Safety Corps / Restoration Councils are design agendas pending sourced modules. These are **doctrine and research agenda**, not ratified Declaration amendments.

## Policy implications (not yet resolved)

Phase 2 does **not** write national criminal codes, sentencing grids, or policing statutes. Future proposals must pass both the Constitutional Capitalism Test and the Justice Test (`CC-JTEST-01`–`10`), including least-coercive professional response and confinement-profit screens.
