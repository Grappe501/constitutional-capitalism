## Central question

Can public institutions become technologically modern, understandable, auditable, staffed by capable citizens, and visibly connected to communities — focused on service rather than self-preservation — without either hollowing out capacity or expanding unaccountable bureaucracy?

## Why it matters

Justice and democracy spines fail in practice if agencies, boards, and service systems are opaque, captured, digitally exclusive, or administratively exhausting. Public service is stewardship of a public trust.

## Contrary evidence

- Aggressive headcount cuts can destroy institutional knowledge and service quality.
- Digital-first services can exclude elderly, disabled, rural, poor, and offline residents without human pathways.
- Sunset reviews and credentials can themselves be captured or become barriers to entry.
- Open appointments can be gamed without conflict and qualification standards.
- Performance dashboards can become public-relations theater if failures are omitted.

## Uncertainties

- Optimal civil-service vs at-will mixes by function.
- Measurement of “wait times,” “error rates,” and “public satisfaction” across agencies.
- Scope of quasi-public entities in a complete authority inventory.

Developing doctrine: noble public service; automate routine / elevate human; preserve professional expertise without bureaucratic isolation; sunset/renewal; citizen-centered accounts and visible boards. Not Declaration amendments.
