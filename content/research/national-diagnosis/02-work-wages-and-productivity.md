## Central question

Have wages and productivity moved together for typical U.S. workers, and what do measurement choices change?

## Why it matters

If typical compensation lags productivity for long periods, claims about shared gains from growth require scrutiny.

## Current findings (sourced inventory)

BLS staff analysis documents a **productivity–compensation gap** since the 1970s: productivity and compensation rose together for decades after the 1940s, then diverged at aggregate and many industry levels (`CC-SRC-003`).

EPI’s secondary series (built from BLS/BEA inputs) reports large cumulative divergence since 1979 — e.g., net productivity +61.8% vs typical-worker hourly pay +17.5% through 2020 under EPI’s definitions (`CC-SRC-004`). EPI is labeled advocacy-affiliated secondary and must not stand alone.

Gap **size** depends on average compensation vs median/typical-worker pay, benefits inclusion, and price deflators. Causal attribution (technology, globalization, institutions, market power) remains debated.

## What the data directly establishes

1. BLS analysis documents a productivity–compensation gap since the 1970s after earlier decades of co-movement (`CC-SRC-003`).
2. In many industries studied for 1987–2015, compensation lagged productivity (`CC-SRC-003`).
3. EPI’s secondary methodology reports large cumulative divergence since 1979 for typical-worker pay vs net productivity (`CC-SRC-004`) — secondary, not a substitute for BLS.
4. The data do **not** establish a single causal decomposition (technology vs globalization vs institutions vs market power).

## What may reasonably be inferred

- Typical workers may not have shared productivity gains one-for-one since the 1970s — **inference** whose magnitude is measurement-sensitive (`CC-SRC-003`, `CC-SRC-004` opposing notes).
- Industry heterogeneity implies national averages can misstate local labor-market experience — **plausible**.
- Employer concentration suppressing wages in some markets (`CC-CLAIM-015`) is a research hypothesis, not an established finding here.

## What Constitutional Capitalism normatively concludes

Persistent divergence between productivity and typical pay is relevant to whether broad prosperity is shared. That relevance is **normative motivation for research**, not a wage rule, bargaining mandate, or constitutional verdict. No wage-policy package is locked in Phase 2.

- Not every sector shows the same gap (`CC-SRC-003` industry heterogeneity).
